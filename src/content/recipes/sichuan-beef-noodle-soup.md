### What Makes It Sichuan?

- **Doubanjiang** (fermented broad bean and chili paste): savoury, funky backbone
- **Sichuan peppercorns**: citrusy, tongue-tingling numbing effect
- **Chili oil**: fat-soluble capsaicin delivery system
- Aromatics layered in stages for controlled extraction

If you skip doubanjiang, you are making spicy beef soup. Not Sichuan.

---

## Ingredients

> [!info] Serves 4

### Broth and Beef

- 1-1.2 kg beef shank or chuck, cut large
- 2 tbsp neutral oil
- 3 tbsp doubanjiang
- 1 tbsp tomato paste
- 3 cloves garlic, smashed
- 3 cm ginger, sliced
- 2 star anise
- 1 small cinnamon stick
- 1 tsp Sichuan peppercorns, lightly crushed
- 1-2 dried Chinese chilies
- 1 tbsp soy sauce
- 1 tbsp Shaoxing wine
- 1.5 L beef stock, or water plus a good stock cube
- 1 tsp sugar

### To Serve

- Fresh wheat noodles, alkaline if possible
- Blanched bok choy or choy sum
- Chili oil
- Sliced spring onions
- Optional: preserved mustard greens, ya cai

---

## Method

### 1. Brown the Beef Properly

Dry it. Sear hard in batches. You want fond, the browned bits stuck to the pot. That is Maillard reaction: amino acids plus sugars creating hundreds of flavour compounds. Do not crowd the pan.

Remove beef.

### 2. Build the Flavour Base in Fat

Lower heat. Add doubanjiang and tomato paste. Fry 2-3 minutes until oil turns red and aromatic. You are blooming fat-soluble flavours.

Add garlic, ginger, and spices. Toast briefly, 30 seconds max. Spices burn easily.

### 3. Deglaze and Simmer

Add Shaoxing wine. Scrape up fond. Return beef. Add stock, soy sauce, and sugar.

Simmer gently 1.5-2 hours, or pressure cook 45 min. You want collagen breakdown into gelatin. That is body and lip-smacking texture.

### 4. Adjust Málà Balance

Taste.

Too flat? Add soy or salt. Too sharp? Add sugar. Not numbing? Bloom more crushed Sichuan pepper in hot oil and stir in. Not spicy enough? Chili oil.

Precision beats bravado.

### 5. Noodles and Assembly

Cook noodles separately. Always separately. Otherwise starch muddies your broth.

Bowl = noodles -> beef -> ladle broth -> greens -> chili oil -> spring onions.

---

## The Science Bit

- **Gelatin from shank** thickens broth without starch.
- **Fat carries capsaicin**, so chili oil increases perceived heat.
- **Sichuan peppercorns contain hydroxy-alpha-sanshool**, which activates touch receptors rather than pain receptors. That is why it numbs instead of burns.
- **Doubanjiang adds glutamates**, amplifying savoury perception.

This dish is controlled chaos: spice, savoury depth, aromatic volatility, tactile numbness.

---

## If You Want to Push It Further

- Add a spoon of beef marrow to the broth for absurd richness.
- Dry-toast peppercorns before crushing for more citrus aroma.
- Add a splash of black vinegar at the end for lift.
- Rest overnight. Like most braises, it improves dramatically.

Sichuan food is not just "spicy Chinese." It is a carefully engineered sensory experience.

The interesting frontier here is how different ratios of málà affect perceived richness and satiety. Adjusting peppercorn levels changes how your brain interprets the heat. That interplay between chemistry and perception is where this dish gets fascinating.
